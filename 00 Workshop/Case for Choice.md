# Why This, For This Cohort

## What they already do well

The brief says it plainly: this cohort is fluent, daily, ad hoc AI users. They already use AI to work through papers, summarizing, asking questions, pulling out what's relevant. If I taught "how to ask AI to summarize a paper," I'd be teaching something they already know how to do. That would waste the best 30 minutes I get with them.

## What's actually missing

The brief names the gap directly, twice: they aren't systematic, and they don't have a good sense of where AI helps versus where it quietly leads them astray. Literature synthesis is where that gap costs the most, for four reasons specific to this program.

1. **It happens at the start of a 13-week fellowship**, before a team has enough domain intuition to sanity-check what AI tells them. A wrong or overstated claim absorbed in week one can steer a whole research direction, and by the time it surfaces, maybe at the paper-writing stage, or worse, from a reviewer, it's expensive to unwind.
2. **The failure is quiet, not obvious.** Bad code throws an error. A missing citation looks wrong. An AI overstating how far a result applies, or mixing up two related terms, reads as completely normal. Nothing about it signals "check this." That's the exact "quietly leads astray" pattern the brief calls out, and it's the failure this cohort is worst placed to catch, because their AI fluency has taught them to trust fluent output.
3. **It compounds, and not just within one paper.** Everything downstream in a research project, the hypothesis, the experiment design, the framing in the eventual paper, inherits whatever the literature synthesis got wrong. Fixing a bad habit in week one pays off for 13 weeks. Fixing it in week ten doesn't.
4. **A single reading session isn't the real unit of value here, a fellowship's worth of reading is.** This is why the workflow produces a note in a linked vault (Obsidian or equivalent), not a chat transcript. A verified synthesis from week one is only worth something in week ten if the fellow can actually find it and see how it connects to what they've read since. A pile of separate AI chats doesn't do that. A linked note does.

## What I considered and rejected

- **A code-review habit for AI-generated code.** A real gap, and a close second choice. But this cohort already writes in a mixed hand-written and AI-generated style every day, so they already have some instinct for checking AI's code. They don't have the same instinct for checking AI's reading of a paper.
- **Persistent context files (CLAUDE.md-style) for their own repos.** Useful, but it makes something they're already good at a bit faster. It doesn't touch the actual gap the brief points to.
- **A general habit of "reviewing how you used AI."** Too vague for 30 minutes with a technical room. It would land as advice, not a workflow, and the brief specifically warns against exactly that kind of loose, one-off practice.

Literature synthesis is also the one option that helps all 20 fellows the same amount, regardless of their specific research topic. Every fellow reads papers, every week. A code-review habit or a context-file convention only pays off for fellows whose workflow happens to match it.

## Keeping it tight

The workshop teaches one thing: Extract, then Verify, then Synthesize, and the habit of checking before trusting. The Obsidian angle isn't a second thing to teach, it's just where the output of that one workflow lives so it doesn't evaporate. I'm not spending workshop time teaching Obsidian itself, fellows who don't use it can use the same template as plain files.
